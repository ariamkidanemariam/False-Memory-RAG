Cognitive Reserve Moderates Older Adults’ Memory Errors in 
Autobiographical Reality Monitoring Task
Kyle R. Kraemer, Tasnuva Enam, and Ian M. McDonough
Department of Psychology, The University of Alabama;
Abstract
False memory rates differ in individuals with high versus low cognitive reserve and between 
young-old and old-old age groups. Here we tested how two types of false memory (false alarms to 
new items and source memory) in two age groups differed with cognitive reserve. Subjects were 
presented with words and either instructed to generate a past event from their memory associated 
with the word or to imagine a future event associated with the word. At test, participants were 
instructed to determine whether the event was a past, future, or new event. Results showed overall 
false memory rates were lower for young-old adults and those with high reserve. Critically, low 
cognitive reserve was most associated with source memory errors in young-old but not old-old 
adults. Reflecting the opposite pattern, false alarms to new items were most associated with low 
cognitive reserve for old-old, but not young-old adults. These results seem to suggest two different 
classes of false memories in old age. That is, cognitive reserve was most protective for familiar 
lures in earlier stages of old age, whereas it was most protective for new lures in later stages of old 
age. These results support the idea that retrieval monitoring deteriorates with age, potentially due 
to declines in working memory capacity, but that the decline may be attenuated by cognitive 
reserve. Furthermore, we suggest that different levels of working memory capacity may be 
required for monitoring source memory versus item memory, leading to differential effects of 
cognitive reserve depending upon age.
Keywords
autobiographical memory; false memories; older adults; cognitive reserve; reality monitoring
Introduction
The ability to correctly remember the past is vital to being able to function effectively. 
However, research has thoroughly established that older adults (typically 60–65+ years of 
age) possess poorer memory ability than younger adults (typically 18–30 years of age), and 
that diminished memory in older adults frequently leads to deficits in independent 
functioning (Cahn-Weiner et al., 2007; Giebel, Challis, & Montaldi, 2015; Overdorp,

Kessels, Claassen, & Oosterman, 2016). While much aging research focuses on the fact that 
older adults forget more information than younger adults, memory errors can also take the 
form of false memories. False memories occur when a person believes they experienced an 
event that they either did not experience or experienced differently from the original event 
(Roediger & McDermott, 1995). On average, older adults produce about twice as many false 
memories as younger adults (for a meta-analysis, see McCabe, Roediger, McDaniel, & 
Balota, 2009). These false memories could create many problems for older adults if, for 
example, they believe that they paid bills that were not actually paid, took medication that 
was not actually taken, or made an agreement that they did not actually make.
One explanation for the increase in false memories in older adults is that older adults rely 
more on familiarity to make decisions about their previous memories than do younger adults 
(Norman & Schacter 1997; Naveh-Benjamin 2000; Yonelinas, 2002). According to the dual- 
process model of memory, people decide about the occurrence of previous events by 
evaluating two types of information—familiarity and recollection. Familiarity refers to an 
automatic process of memory that provides immediate information about whether the 
current item has been encountered before, while recollection refers to a more effortful, 
conscious process that involves the recall of actual details of a previous encounter 
(Yonelinas, 2002). Behavioral studies have consistently demonstrated that aging selectively 
impairs recollection-based processes, but spares familiarity-based processes (e.g., Jennings 
& Jacoby, 1993; Koen & Yonelinas, 2014; Pitarque et al., 2016). Neuroimaging studies have 
yielded converging evidence with behavorioal studies. Older adults exhibit reduced 
activation of the prefrontal and hippocampal brain regions typically more associated with 
recollection compared with younger adults (e.g., Cabeza 2002; Logan, Sanders, Morris, & 
Buckner, 2002; Morcom, Good, Frackowiak, & Rugg, 2003). These reductions in brain 
activity in recollection-related brain regions are often accompanied by a shift of activation to 
brain regions associated with familiarity-based processes (e.g., Daselaar, Fleck, Dobbins, 
Madden, & Cabeza, 2006)
While the differences in memory between younger and older adults have been widely 
investigated, relatively fewer studies have focused on age differences within the older 
population. In particular, sometimes distinctions are made between young-old (typically 
between ages 60–74 years) and old-old (typically above age of 76 years) adults (e.g., 
McGinnis, 2012; Wang, Kruggel, & Rugg, 2009). Although the specific ages of these two 
categories differ by study, consistent patterns still appear when comparing young-old and 
old-old adults. Interestingly, while old-old adults seem to possess consistently poorer source 
memory than young-old adults, several studies report no difference between these ages for 
item memory (Cansino, 2009; Cohen & Faulkner, 1989). These findings are in line with the 
aforementioned declines in recollection and sparing of familiarity. Generally, while both 
source and item memory may decline with age, old-old adults show greater impairment in 
source memory compared to item memory (Cansino et al., 2013; Erngrund, Mántylá & 
Nilsson, 1996). Thus, advanced age seems to be associated with particularly poorer 
recollection, and thus, the potential for an increased reliance on familiarity compared to 
young-old adults.

Retrieval Monitoring
While the increased reliance on familiarity over recollection may partially explain the 
increase in false memories for older adults, the increase may also be due to a decline in 
retrieval monitoring ability. That is, when attempting to retrieve a particular event, a person 
may evaluate the contents of the retrieved memory for consistency and plausibility to inform 
the veracity of the memory signal (Gallo & Lampinen, 2015). Examples of retrieval 
monitoring include recollection rejection (a.k.a. “recall-to-reject”; e.g. Brainerd, Reyna, 
Wright, & Mojardin, 2003; Gallo, 2004) and the distinctiveness heuristic (Schacter, Israel, & 
Racine, 1999; for review, see Gallo, 2013), which involve the rejection of familiar items 
based on, respectively, recollected items and expectations of a highly distinctive memory 
trace. Because retrieval monitoring is critical to accurate memory performance (Johnson, 
Hashtroudi, & Lindsay, 1993), deficits within retrieval monitoring ability may partially 
explain the increase in false memories experienced by older adults.
The relative impairment of retrieval monitoring in older adults has been shown through both 
behavioral and neuroimaging studies. For example, when elaboration was used to increase 
recollected features of experiences, only younger adults were able to take advantage of the 
increase in recollected features to correspondingly reduce their source memory errors, likely 
due to older adults possessing deficits in retrieval monitoring (McDonough & Gallo, 2013). 
Furthermore, while younger adults show greater activation in the DLPFC for more difficult 
memory decisions (words compared with pictures), which indicates that retrieval monitoring 
is engaged according to the difficulty of the task, older adults show no difference in DLPFC 
activation for these different tasks. This evidence may indicate that retrieval monitoring 
dysfunction is at least partially responsible for the differences in source memory observed 
between older and younger adults (McDonough, Wong, & Gallo, 2013).
Cognitive Reserve
Although memory dysfunction can be extremely prohibitive to the function of older adults, 
some recourse can be found for those who have higher cognitive reserve. Cognitive reserve 
refers to the observed disconnect between the neural pathology associated with aging and 
dementia and the expected cognitive symptomology. Studies have repeatedly shown that 
certain characteristics, such as having high socioeconomic status or high levels of education, 
are related to reduced cognitive symptomology (Stern, 2009). In one well-known study, the 
linguistic complexity of a person’s writing at a young age was found to predict the cognitive 
function of that person in old age, as well as their chance of developing Alzheimer’s disease 
(Snowdon et al., 1996). Many proxies of cognitive reserve have been considered, including 
educational level, occupational status, and cognitively stimulating leisure activities—all of 
which possess a significant positive relationship with general cognition in healthy older 
adults (Opdebeeck, Martyr & Clare, 2016).
However, when specifically assessing how cognitive reserve impacts recollection and 
familiarity, studies provide mixed evidence. For example, Pitarque and colleagues (2016) 
discovered that older adults with lower cognitive reserve committed significantly more false 
alarms and claimed to be “remembered” than those with higher cognitive reserve. This

While a rich amount of research has been conducted on false memory within the field of 
cognitive aging, relatively few studies have specifically investigated the differential false 
memory rates for tasks assessing item and source memory within older adults by comparing 
young-old and old-old adults, and to our knowledge, none have investigated how these forms 
of memory errors might be impacted by cognitive reserve within these age groups. This 
question is critical to understand how older adults can protect both recollection-based and 
familiarity-based processes into advanced age.
The Current Study
For the current study, data were reanalyzed from both Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 of 
McDonough and Gallo (2013) to determine differences between young-old and old-old 
adults on memory errors within a reality monitoring task, and how cognitive reserve might 
moderate these errors. While McDonough and Gallo (2013) previously assessed differences 
in memory accuracy between college-aged younger adults (Mage=20.37) and older adults 
generally (Mage= 76.24), no analyses comparing memory errors among older adults or 
cognitive reserve status were previously reported from this data, nor did any previous 
analyses compare error types between studied vs. non-studied lures. For the current study, 
we hypothesized that old-old adults would make more memory errors to non-studied lures 
than young-old adults, as well as more memory errors than young-old adults from 
misidentifying lures that were originally studied in a different source; however, we also 
hypothesized that these effects would be ameliorated in older adults with higher cognitive 
reserve.
Method
Participants
A total of 69 older adults (female N = 49) participated in the study. A median split was used 
to separate the participants into young-old (N = 34; age, 60 – 75) and old-old (N = 35; age, 
76 – 92), an age division similar to that used in previous studies (McGinnis, 2012; Wang et 
al., 2009). Participants were recruited from the Chicago community and were compensated 
with $20 for their time. To ensure suitable mental health, older adults passed a screening for 
a history of mental illness, showed no signs of depression (Geriatric Depression Scale, 
young-old M = 3.97, SD = 3.97; old-old M = 3.31, SD = 3.53) or deficits in cognitive 
functioning (Mini-Mental State Examination, young-old M = 28.38, SD = 1.86; old-old M = 
28.43, SD = 1.38). Cognitive reserve was measured using the vocabulary portion of the 
Shipley Institute of Living Scale (SILS; Shipley & Burlingame, 1941), and a median split 
was used to separate older adults into higher cognitive reserve (SILS > 35, N = 39) and

Materials
Stimuli used for the current study included 32 positive words from emotional word norms 
(Bradley & Lang, 1999) and 32 neutral words from McDonough and Gallo (2010). Although 
there were 64 words in total, 16 words were left out of the study phase for each participant 
and were instead used as non-studied lures at test. The individual words used for each 
portion of the study were counterbalanced across participants. All stimuli were presented to 
participants on a computer through the Cogent2000 toolbox in Matlab.
Procedure
All participants completed the experiment in two phases—a study phase and a test phase that 
occurred 24 hours later. Before each phase, the experimenter read the instructions aloud to 
each participant and observed performance to verify a correct understanding of the tasks. 
During the study phase, each participant was presented with a total of 48 different cue words 
under two different sets of instructions. For the past event generation block, participants 
were presented with 32 words and instructed to recall a past memory related to that cue 
word. For the future event generation block, participants were presented with 32 cue words 
and were instructed to imagine a future event related to the cue (note that 16 words 
overlapped between the blocks and were dubbed “both” items, while 16 were unique to each 
block). For each past or future event, after receiving the cue word, participants were given 
one minute to recall (past) or imagine (future) as much as they could about the event and 
verbally provide one-word descriptions of the event, including a title, who was in the event, 
and the location of the event (these responses were recorded by the experimenter to be used 
in the test phase).
Although every participant completed both a past event generation block and a future event 
generation block, the presence of an “elaborative” condition differed according to 
participant. That is, the study phase was further split into either a past/elaborate event 
generation or a past/non-elaborate event generation (and the same for future event 
generation). In the elaborative conditions, participants received an extra 1.5 minutes of 
elaboration to describe additional sensory details for past events or to describe details of the 
future plan for the future event. The current analyses collapsed across the elaborative and 
non-elaborative conditions because we were primarily interested in overall memory errors 
among different ages and cognitive reserve levels, regardless of the degree of elaboration for 
individual items (for a detailed discussion of the effects of elaboration, see McDonough and 
Gallo, 2013).
The test phase occurred 24 hours later. Each participant was presented with a total of 64 
events—16 previously generated past events (single-source), 16 previously generated future 
events (single-source), 16 events generated in both the past and future conditions (“both” 
items), and 16 new events—before being asked to make a memory judgment. While all

On these tests, participants could make two types of memory errors, depending upon the lure 
with which they were presented: studied or non-studied lures. We define source memory 
errors as those occurring when participants incorrectly endorsed a studied lure as having 
been previously generated in the wrong source (e.g., claiming an actual past event was an 
event generated in the future condition). We define item memory errors as those occurring 
when participants incorrectly endorsed a non-studied lure as having been previously 
generated in any source.
Results
Participant Characteristics
A 2 (Age Group: Young-Old, Old-Old) × 2 (Cognitive Reserve: Higher, Lower) analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the participant characteristics including sex, 
education, MMSE, and GDS. Not surprisingly, these ANOVAs resulted in a main effect of 
Cognitive Reserve group for education, F (1,69) = 9.56, MSE = 6.63, p = .003, ηp2 = .13, 
such that older adults in the Higher Cognitive Reserve group had higher education than older 
adults in the Lower Cognitive Reserve group. No other main effects or interactions were 
significant (all p’s > .09).
Effects of Targets
Although our main analysis concerned memory errors, we first present the results on hits for 
completeness. We conducted a 2 (Age Group: Young-Old, Old-Old) × 2 (Cognitive Reserve: 
Higher, Lower) × 2 (Target: Both, Single-source) mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) on 
proportion of hits across the memory tests. This analysis resulted in no significant effects 
(all ps > .10).
Effects of Lures
We next focus on our primary analysis of interest: memory errors. We conducted a 2 (Age 
Group: Young-Old, Old-Old) × 2 (Cognitive Reserve: High, Low) × 2 (Lure: Studied, Non-
studied) mixed ANOVA on proportion of memory errors across the criterial recollection 
tests. This analysis resulted in a main effect of Age Group, F (1,65) = 4.47, MSE = .091, p 
= .038, ηp2= .064, such that the Young-Old committed fewer memory errors than the Old-

Discussion
The results support our hypothesis that old-old adults commit more memory errors generally 
than young-old adults. Furthermore, the expectation that older adults with higher cognitive 
reserve would commit fewer errors than older adults with lower cognitive reserve was also 
met. Finally, our key finding was that the protective effects of cognitive reserve differed with 
age such that false memories to studied lures from the incorrect source were reduced in the 
young-old with higher reserve and false memories to new, non-studied lures were reduced in 
the old-old with higher reserve.
While previous studies have examined how false memories to studied lures and non-studied 
lures change with age more generally (e.g. Cansino et al., 2013), our study is the first of 
which we are aware to examine how cognitive reserve affects the increase in each of these 
types of false memories according to age among older adults. Our results suggest that 
cognitive reserve relates to false memory in different ways for people of different age 
groups. For young-old adults, cognitive reserve maintains prevention of source memory 
errors, allowing young-old adults to more accurately distinguish between items that were 
experienced in different contexts (recollection-based processes). In contrast, for old-old 
adults, cognitive reserve maintains prevention of item memory errors, allowing old-old 
adults to more accurately identify items that have been not previously experienced 
(familiarity-based processes).
These findings have many implications for the aging memories of older adults. Although the 
prefrontal cortex experiences some of the largest changes in structure and function due to 
aging (Peters, Sethares, & Moss, 1998; Raz et al., 1997; Rossi et al., 2004), cognitive reserve 
may preserve PFC function. Older adults with low cognitive reserve score lower on tasks 
that are mediated by the DLPFC such as tasks involving reasoning, working memory, and 
verbal fluency (Roldán-Tapia, García, Cánovas, & León, 2012). The results of the current 
study imply that, for older adults with high cognitive reserve, important prefrontal areas such

consistent with an effect specific to retrieval monitoring.
Importantly, working memory may play a key role in the process of retrieval monitoring, 
helping to explain the pattern seen in the current results—namely, the differing effects of 
cognitive reserve according to age and type of memory process. Considering that source 
memory is typically a more difficult task than item memory, the shift with age in the type of 
memory error most affected by cognitive reserve could be due to specific levels of working 
memory being necessary for each particular memory process. That is, while cognitive 
reserve seems to protect young-old adults from increasing errors in source memory, after 
episodic memory declines with age, old-old adults may no longer possess the working 
memory capacity for accurate source memory regardless of cognitive reserve status. This 
idea is consistent with findings that source memory decreases more rapidly with age than 
item memory (Erngrund, Mántylá & Nilsson, 1996). In contrast, young-old adults may 
retain sufficient levels of working memory capacity necessary for item memory regardless of 
cognitive reserve status, but after cognitive decline with advanced age, the working memory 
capacity of old-old adults necessary for item memory may be impaired unless this process is 
attenuated due to cognitive reserve.
This line of reasoning is supported by our observation that old-old adults with high cognitive 
reserve somewhat resemble young-old adults with low cognitive reserve for both item and 
source memory errors (see Figure 1), indicating the potential that cognitive reserve leads to a 
general attenuation of memory decline with age.
The present results could also be considered as fitting within the activation-monitoring 
framework for false memories (Roediger, Watson, McDermott, & Gallo, 2001; McDermott 
& Watson, 2001). When considering the roles of recollection and familiarity within the 
activation/monitoring framework, separate memory traces may be monitored differently, 
resulting in different neural requirements. For example, more difficult, previously studied 
lures have led to higher levels of PFC activation than relatively simpler, non-studied lures 
(McDonough et al., 2013). Evidence from neuroimaging studies are consistent with the idea 
that monitoring of recollection-based memory traces is more difficult than monitoring of 
familiarity-based memory traces, and may depend more heavily upon working memory 
capacity (Oberauer, 2005). Thus, the consequences of cognitive reserve’s relative 
preservation of working memory capacity may differ according to the neural resources 
needed for a particular type of retrieval monitoring.
Finally, the complex interaction that we found between age, cognitive reserve, and type of 
memory error may partially explain findings within the literature regarding cognitive reserve 
that previously appeared contradictory. Pitarque and colleagues (2016) showed that older 
adults of higher and lower cognitive reserve status possessed relatively equal false alarms for 
“know” judgments, indicating that familiarity-based processes may not be protected by 
cognitive reserve. However, Sales and colleagues (2014) showed that those with low 
cognitive reserve made more false alarms than those with high cognitive reserve. In light of

Conclusion
The current study underscores the importance of cognitive reserve for minimizing false 
memories later in life. Item and source memory are foundational cognitive abilities that 
make possible the ability to appropriately care for oneself and to meaningfully relate to the 
experiences of others, leading to a more fulfilling life. Thus, as much as people are able to 
build cognitive reserve in preparation for advanced age, they should do so in order to 
maintain optimal quality of life. Furthermore, the current study suggests that cognitive 
reserve may preserve retrieval monitoring abilities in older adults. As the aging population 
increases, the ability to predict the difficulties that specific older adults are likely to 
encounter is increasingly important to provide appropriate care for those individuals. Thus, 
knowledge of the specific cognitive abilities likely to be affected may be of use to 
physicians, families, and care providers for the advanced aging population. For example, 
adults over 75 with low cognitive reserve appear to be at the greatest risk for losing the 
ability to distinguish between events that did or did not occur. The current examination of 
the effects of cognitive reserve may therefore contribute not only to the understanding of 
how memory changes with age, but also to the ability to live well into advanced age.
