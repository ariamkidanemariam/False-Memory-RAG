Creating Emotional False Recollections: Perceptual 
Recombination and Conceptual Fluency Mechanisms
Manoj K. Doss1,2, Jamila K. Picart1, David A. Gallo1
1Department of Psychology, University of Chicago
2Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Science, Johns Hopkins University
Abstract
We investigated the impact of two hypothetical mechanisms of episodic memory reconstruction—
perceptual recombination and conceptual fluency—on objectively measured recollection accuracy 
and false recollections of neutral and emotional stimuli. Participants encoded negative, neutral, 
and positive pictures depicting objects and scenes (i.e., target pictures), each accompanied with a 
descriptive verbal label (e.g., “boy crying at funeral”, “wooden basket on floor,” “four 
chimpanzees laughing together”). Next, they encoded fragmented pictures of some of the scenes 
they did and did not earlier see (perceptual misinformation), or they received multiple 
presentations of the corresponding verbal labels (conceptual misinformation). Recollection of 
target pictures was then tested, using labels as retrieval cues. We had three key findings in each of 
two experiments. First, as in our prior work, both perceptual and conceptual misinformation 
significantly increased false recollection judgments of nonstudied pictures, including high 
confidence errors. These effects implicate perceptual recombination and conceptual fluency 
mechanisms. Second, these misinformation effects generalized across all emotional categories, 
implicating separable roles of these two mechanisms on emotional recollections. Finally, 
conceptual misinformation was less likely to influence negative than neutral recollection errors, 
providing new evidence that emotion can improve retrieval monitoring accuracy and reduce false 
memories based on conceptual fluency (i.e., an emotional distinctiveness heuristic).
Keywords
emotional memory; false memory; false recollection; perceptual recombination; conceptual 
fluency
Introduction
The recollection of emotional events is often accompanied with a subjective sense of 
vividness but research indicates that emotional events are susceptible to false recollection, or 
the subjective experience of conscious recalling some detail or event that did not actually 
happen. One complexity of this research literature is that emotional events have sometimes 
been found to be more prone to false recollection and other times less prone for reasons that

are not yet fully understood. For example, on episodic memory tests for words or pictures, a 
common finding is greater false recognition of nonstudied emotional than neutral items (e.g., 
Bessette-Symons, 2018; Bookbinder & Brainerd, 2017; Brainerd et al., 2008; Gallo, Foster, 
& Johnson, 2009; Gong, Xiao, & Wang, 2016; White, et al., 2014). Such findings have been 
attributed to the propensity for emotional events to share conceptual or semantic features or 
otherwise share an “emotional gist”. As a result, emotional studied events are more likely to 
activate nonstudied emotional events relative to neutral events, increasing false recognition 
for emotional events.
In contrast to these findings, it also has been found that emotional events can be less prone 
to memory distortion than neutral events, at least for negative emotional content (e.g., Farris 
& Toglia, 2018; Kensinger et al., 2007; Kensinger & Schacter, 2006; Pesta, Murphy, & 
Sanders, 2001; Zheng et al., 2018). According to Schacter, Gallo, and Kensinger (2007), 
people often expect emotional events to be recollected more vividly, and expecting vivid or 
perceptually detailed recollections might help reduce false recognition of nonstudied events 
that only feel familiar or vaguely recollected (i.e., an emotional distinctiveness heuristic). 
Although some studies relevant to this issue have conflated emotional and conceptual or 
semantic distinctiveness (Pesta et al., 2001), Choi, Kensinger, and Rajaram (2013) avoided 
these issues by testing recognition for emotional pictures and controlling for conceptual 
relatedness across emotional categories, and they found that emotional items were equally or 
less likely to be falsely recognized as emotionally neutral items on a recognition memory 
test. This study, therefore, provided stronger support for the emotional distinctiveness 
hypothesis, although the effect was not obtained in all of the experiments.
As articulated by Bookbinder and Brainerd (2016), emotion can simultaneously impact 
multiple (and sometimes competing) cognitive processes, and emotion itself has different 
facets, thereby making generalizations across areas of emotional memory research difficult. 
For example, the aforementioned research indicates that emotional events can be more prone 
to gist-based influences that can drive false remembering, but these events also may trigger 
heightened memorial expectations that might reduce false remembering. Because of this 
complexity, researchers have started to move away from asking questions like “are 
emotional events more or less susceptible to memory distortion?” and, instead, they are now 
asking how the multiple processes that contribute to memory accuracy and distortion might 
differentially contribute to emotional and neutral events. In this spirit, the current 
experiments aimed to investigate the extent that two hypothetical mechanisms of memory 
reconstruction might differentially contribute to the recollection of emotional and neutral 
events.
Two Reconstructive Mechanisms
Doss, Bluestone, and Gallo (2016) proposed two hypothetical mechanisms of memory 
construction that occur during retrieval. The first mechanism is perceptual recombination. 
According to this hypothesis, the erroneous perceptual content that characterizes false 
recollections can originate from previously experienced events that are stored in episodic 
memory. These perceptual details can be fragmented or dissociated from their instantiating 
events in memory, providing fuel for reconstructive processes that cause false memories.

The idea that different kinds of features or details can be erroneously confused across 
different events in memory has been proposed by many researchers (e.g., Dodson, Bawa, & 
Slotnick, 2007; Gallo & Roediger, 2003; Lampinen, Neuschatz, & Payne, 1999; Lyle & 
Johnson, 2006), although the perceptual recombination hypothesis focuses exclusively on 
perceptual features from prior experience to differentiate it from a conceptual fluency 
mechanism.
The second mechanism is conceptual fluency. According to this hypothesis, the erroneous 
perceptual content of false recollections originates from mental imagery, as opposed to 
actually perceived events. People might mentally imagine events and then later falsely 
retrieve them, or they might mentally imagine events as part of the retrieval process itself, as 
when someone uses mental imagery to cue or trigger their memories and monitor them for 
accuracy. In either case, if these mentally imagined perceptual details are generated in a way 
that makes them seem familiar or fluent, potentially due to increased semantic activation or 
conceptual priming, then they also might provide the fuel for a reconstructive process that 
causes false memories. The idea that conceptual fluency can be misattributed to prior 
experience has been proposed by many researchers (e.g., Gallo & Roediger, 2003; Kurilla & 
Westerman, 2008; Rajaram & Geraci, 2000; Whittlesea, 2002), although these ideas 
sometimes focus on familiarity-based false recognition as opposed to perceptually-detailed 
false recollection.
Doss et al. (2016) reported the first set of experiments that were specifically designed to 
isolate perceptual recombination and conceptual fluency effects on the false recollection of 
nonstudied pictures. To do this, Doss et al. developed a multi-phase misinformation task that 
involved the presentation of two kinds of misinformation: perceptual and conceptual. In the 
first phase (encoding phase), participants studied verbal labels corresponding to pictures of 
objects (e.g., “wine,” “puppy,” “lemon”) with half of these labels followed by the 
corresponding picture. These items would later serve as targets on the recollection test, 
whereas on the other half of trials, the verbal label was presented alone, later to serve as 
studied lures. In the next phase (misinformation phase), stimuli were presented to distort 
memory, though from the participants’ perspective, this was just additional information. 
There were two kinds of misinformation: perceptual misinformation (nonstudied pictures 
that had been scrambled) and conceptual misinformation (nonstudied verbal labels). Finally, 
an episodic memory test was given (cued recollection phase). On this test, participants were 
presented with all of the previously studied verbal labels as memory cues, and they were 
asked to press “yes” only if they recollected having studied an associated target picture 
during the encoding phase.
Doss et al. (2016) reported three key effects for present purposes. First, false alarms were 
greater for lures associated with conceptual misinformation compared to a no 
misinformation foil baseline, showing that the activation of the concept at the semantic level 
(without picture features) was sufficient to increase false alarms. Second, false alarms on the 
recollection test were greater for lures associated with perceptual than conceptual 
misinformation, even though each kind of lure had been earlier studied and hence was highly 
familiar. Doss et al. argued that the presentation of fragmented pictures fueled a perceptual 
recombination process that, in turn, increased false alarms above the conceptual

The Current Experiments
The main goal of the current experiments was to investigate the impact of perceptual 
recombination and conceptual fluency on neutral and emotional recollections. Doss et al. 
(2016) found significant effects of perceptual and conceptual misinformation on emotionally 
neutral recollections, and here we aimed to replicate and extend these effects across a 
broader range of picture stimuli, including emotionally positive and negative as well as 
neutral pictures of common objects and scenes. Such replication and extension would further 
establish the robust and reliable nature of the empirical effects reported by Doss et al. and 
provide a test for our specific hypotheses. Based on the emotional memory literature, we had 
two contrasting (but not mutually inconsistent) hypotheses: the emotional enhancement 
hypothesis and the emotional distinctiveness heuristic hypothesis.
According to the emotional enhancement hypothesis, the same processes that tend to 
improve memory encoding for emotional over neutral items (e.g., enhanced attention, 
elaboration, and physiological arousal) also would benefit the encoding of misinformation, 
making this misinformation more accessible at retrieval and hence, more likely to drive false 
recollections. All other factors being equal, this hypothesis predicted greater perceptual 
recombination and conceptual fluency effects on false alarms for the emotional relative to 
neutral items.
According to the emotional distinctiveness heuristic hypothesis, people are more likely to 
expect perceptually detailed recollections for emotionally arousing events compared to 
neutral ones, thereby helping them avoid recollection errors to new events that are only 
familiar or vaguely recollected. This hypothesis predicted reduced perceptual recombination 
and conceptual fluency effects on false alarms for emotional items relative to neutral items, 
again all other factors being equal. Alternatively, such an emotional distinctiveness heuristic 
might help people monitor all sources of memory distortion (e.g., misinformation for studied 
lures and idiosyncratic item associations for nonstudied foils), thereby reducing false alarms 
to all emotional lures regardless of the misinformation condition. In each case, this 
hypothesis claims that emotion will increase a retrieval monitoring process to reduce false 
recollections, in contrast to the emotional enhancement hypothesis.

Experiments 1 and 2. Each of these two experiments used the same modified version of the 
Doss et al. (2016) procedures, described below in the context of Experiment 1. By design,

Experiment 1
Experiment 1 used a modification of the Doss et al. (2016) procedure to investigate the 
susceptibility of emotionally negative, neutral, and positive recollections to perceptual 
recombination and conceptual fluency. To do this, we constructed a new set of neutral and 
emotional picture stimuli, and we developed a four-phase misinformation task.
With respect to the stimuli, our goal with these experiments was to investigate the impact of 
emotion on recollection accuracy and distortion, independent from the semantic or 
conceptual relatedness shared between the pictures and concepts represented in the set of 
stimuli. Prior work has shown that emotional items can be more conceptually related than 
neutral items (e.g., Talmi & Moscovitch, 2004). To the extent that this factor can drive false 
recollection differences between emotional categories (see Gallo et al., 2009), it would 
represent a confound in the current procedure. To avoid this potential confound, the labels 
developed for the current experiments were created so that the same kinds of concepts (e.g., 
people, animals, objects, scenes) would be represented with similar frequency in each of the 
three emotional valence categories (negative, neutral, positive). Moreover, we selected labels 
and stimuli to be conceptually distinct from one another, so that memory errors were 
unlikely to be attributed to participants making label confusion errors between studied and 
nonstudied stimuli.
With respect to the task, there were four phases. The first was the picture encoding phase. 
Participants were presented with emotional and neutral scenes and their verbal labels (these 
would later be targets) intermixed with verbal labels without pictures (these would later be 
lures). The second phase was a perceptual misinformation list, which presented verbal labels 
and scrambled versions of the corresponding picture. The third phase was the conceptual 
misinformation list, which presented verbal labels without any other perceptual information. 
In this way, the familiarity of items presented in the two misinformation lists was quite high, 
requiring recollection to accurately discriminate between them. Importantly, as in Doss et al. 
(2016), misinformation was presented for both targets and lures to preclude a recall-to-reject 
strategy. The final phase was the picture recollection test, which presented labels from the 
first list, as well as brand new labels (nonstudied foils), as possible retrieval cues. On this 
test, participants were instructed to say “yes” if they recollected seeing an intact picture of 
the scene from the first list, regardless of what they may remember from a different list and 
then to rate their confidence.

Participants
Thirty-two individuals (10 males) aged 18- to 22-years-old (M = 19.63, SD = 1.03) 
participated in this study. We chose this sample size because it completed four rounds of 
counterbalancing (see Stimuli section below) and because our previous study with a similar 
design used an N of 28 (Doss et al., 2016). All individuals were native English speakers and 
had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The institutional review board at the University of 
Chicago approved all experimental procedures in this study. Participants were compensated 
for their time with class credit.
Stimuli
Three hundred sixty pictures were selected from both the International Affective Picture 
System (Lang, Bradley, & Cuthbert, 2008) and the Nencki Affective Picture System 
(Marchewka, Żurawski, Jednoróg, & Grabowska, 2014) in an effort to balance the semantic 
relatedness between valence conditions. There were 120 pictures in each valence condition, 
and these pictures had the following mean (SD) normed valences and arousals, respectively: 
negative 3.18 (.53) and 5.87 (.50), neutral 5.15 (.48) and 3.71 (.88), positive 6.92 (.54) and 
5.47 (.41). Negative pictures were more arousing than neutral (95% CI = [1.978, 2.342], 
t(238) = 23.412, p < .001, d = 3.023) and positive (95% CI = [.277, .509], t(238) = 6.692, p 
< .001, d = .864) pictures, and positive pictures were more arousing than neutral pictures 
(95% CI = [1.592, 1.942], t(238) = 19.911, p < .001, d = 2.571). Each picture was ascribed 
to a three- to five-word label to describe the content of the picture (e.g., “orangutan hanging 
from electrical wire”). See Supplemental Materials (SM) showing that the arousal of these 
labels, as well as their corresponding pictures, were rated in accordance with the normed 
arousal ratings of the pictures (i.e., negative > positive > neutral). In order to create the 
stimuli that were presented during the perceptual misinformation phase, the original 360 
pictures were scrambled by segmenting each one into quadrants and then segmenting these 
quadrants further by subdividing them into smaller quadrants. Each smaller quadrant was 
then rotated in a counterclockwise fashion within the larger quadrants (Figure 1). This 
method for scrambling the pictures allows key features described by the label to be 
discerned but also prevents the participant from mistaking the scrambled version of the 
picture for the intact version.
The pictures were divided into 8 lists of 45 pictures (15 of each valence) based on 
conceptual similarities (e.g., food, animals, vehicles). The lists were rotated through the 
experimental conditions in a Latin square design such that for each participant there were 
135 targets (45 of each valence), 135 lures (45 of each valence), and 90 nonstudied foils (30 
of each valence). For both targets and lures, 90 of the 135 items (30 of each valence) were 
presented in the perceptual misinformation phase, and the other 45 items (15 of each 
valence) were presented in the conceptual misinformation phase (see below).
Procedure
The experiment consisted of four phases: an incidental encoding phase, two misinformation 
phases (perceptual and conceptual), and a cued recollection phase separated by a 24-hr delay 
from the preceding three phases. At the beginning of each phase, a researcher read the

instructions to participants, and participants also read the instructions on the computer

During the encoding phase, participants were presented with 270 labels in black font on a 
grey background in random order. Each label was presented for 2,000 ms during which 
participants were instructed to rate its emotional intensity (i.e., arousal) on a 5-point scale. 
Emotional intensity was defined as the strength of the emotional reaction regardless of 
negative or positive content. For half the trials (targets), a picture was presented after the 
label for 2,000 ms during which participants were again to make an emotional intensity 
rating. For the other half of trials (lures), no picture was presented. Across all phases, each 
trial ended with a fixation cross for a random duration that was on average 2,000 ms and 
never shorter than 500 ms.
During the perceptual misinformation phase, participants were presented with 180 
scrambled picture and label pairings in random order. Each trial consisted of the 
simultaneous presentation of a scrambled picture and its corresponding label near the bottom 
of the screen for 2,000 ms. Participants were instructed to mentally unscramble each picture 
described by the label and rate the clarity of their mental image on a five-point scale. Half of 
the scrambled picture and label pairings corresponded to target trials and the other half 
corresponded to lure trials. Labels were the same as those presented during the encoding 
phase.
During the conceptual misinformation phase, participants were presented with 90 labels 
from the encoding phase in random order. Each label was presented for 2,000 ms during 
which participants were instructed to rate the emotional intensity. Each label was presented 
twice to increase familiarity of the conceptual misinformation trials relative to the perceptual 
misinformation (which was only presented once), thereby providing a high-familiarity 
baseline for the perceptual misinformation trials. Half of the labels corresponded to target 
trials and the other half to lure trials. After this phase, participants left the testing room and 
returned the following day for the cued recollection phase.
During the retrieval phase, participants’ picture memory was tested by cueing them with 270 
familiar labels (135 targets and 135 lures) and 90 new labels (nonstudied foils). Participants 
were asked to remember whether they saw the intact version of the picture described by the 
label (“yes”/“no”). Participants were told that recollection of a scrambled picture was 
irrelevant to their recollection of an intact picture because it was possible that both an intact 
and a scrambled picture were presented or just a scrambled picture was presented. This 
procedure was intended to minimize the use of a recall-to-reject strategy. After each memory 
decision, participants were then asked to rate their confidence on a five-point scale. This 
phase was self-paced.

Experiment 1 Results and Discussion
Full data for Experiment 1 can be found in Table 1. In the following results sections, our 
primary analyses are on recollection errors (i.e., false alarms on the recollection task, which 
we assume are indicative of false recollection of nonstudied pictures, although familiarity-
based guessing also may contribute). We show perceptual and conceptual misinformation 
impacted recollection errors across all emotional categories, demonstrating the two key 
effects of Doss et al. (2016) and extending these mechanisms to emotional recollection. We 
also show reduced recollection errors for negative lures compared to positive and neutral 
lures, consistent with an emotional distinctiveness heuristic. There was some evidence that 
this heuristic reduced the impact of the conceptual fluency for negative items, although this 
effect was stronger in Experiment 2. Finally, we also report more traditional measures of 
target-lure discrimination to objectively measure recollection accuracy, and analyses of those 
results are consistent with the analyses of recollection errors.
To measure the potential impact of a perceptual recombination mechanism, we directly 
compared false alarms to the two kinds of studied lures: those that had been studied in the 
perceptual misinformation list (thereby boosting the activation of perceptual and conceptual 
information) and those that had been studied in the conceptual misinformation list (thereby 
boosting the activation of conceptual information alone). Greater false alarms to lures from 
the perceptual misinformation list are taken as evidence for a perceptual recombination 
process while controlling for the possible influence of conceptual information, which is 
assessed using the conceptual misinformation lures as a baseline.

High Confidence Responses
We also considered high confidence false alarms to studied lures (i.e., the top two 
confidence bins). As can be seen in Figure 3, such high confidence false alarms almost never 
occurred for nonstudied foils, though they occurred for about 2 to 3% of lures with 
conceptual misinformation and 9 to 11% of lures with perceptual misinformation. A 3 
(valence: negative, neutral, positive) × 2 (misinformation: perceptual, conceptual) repeated 
measures ANOVA revealed a main effect of misinformation type on false alarm rates (F(1, 
31) = 15.100, p < .001, ηP2 = . 328, owing to perceptual misinformation eliciting greater 
recollection errors than conceptual misinformation. Neither the main effect of valence (F(2, 
62) = .723, p > .250) nor the valence by misinformation interaction were significant (F(2, 
62) = .087, p > .250), demonstrating a perceptual recombination effect for all emotional 
categories. A similar high confidence analysis of conceptual fluency was not conducted 
because of fewer high confidence false alarms to nonstudied foils, though inspection of 
Figure 3 indicates that this mechanism also increased high confidence errors relative to 
baseline for each emotional category.
Recollection Accuracy Analysis
In addition to our primary analysis of false recollection effects, we also calculated target-lure 
discrimination scores as a more traditional measure of recollection accuracy. Within each 
emotional and misinformation condition, recollection accuracy was calculated as the hit 
rates for targets minus the false alarm rates for studied lures (i.e., p(“yes”|target) - p(“yes”|
lure)). Because each of these targets and lures were initially studied in two different lists 
(i.e., the initial label/picture study list and one of the subsequent misinformation lists), they 
should have been highly familiar so that the accurate discrimination between the targets and 
studied lures should have relied primarily on recollection of the target picture.
Analysis of these recollection accuracy scores yielded effects that were consistent with those 
which we have already presented and discussed. A 3 (valence: negative, neutral, positive) ×

Experiment 2
The goal of Experiment 2 was to investigate the impact of perceptual and conceptual 
misinformation when the misinformation occurred on the same day as retrieval instead of 
immediately after the encoding phase as in Experiment 1. This experiment was identical to 
Experiment 1 except the 24-hour delay was introduced before the misinformation phases 
rather than before the retrieval phase. Doss et al. (2016) found that the effect of conceptual 
misinformation on recollection errors was larger when conceptual activation occurred just 
before the test phase as opposed to the day before (cf. Doss, Picart, & Gallo, 2018 in which 
activating conceptual associations, via context reinstatement, prior to memory retrieval at the 
trial level robustly increased high confidence false memories). Because perceptual 
misinformation was not affected by such a timing manipulation, these findings suggest that 
conceptual fluency fades more rapidly over time. This experiment, therefore, provided a 
replication attempt of Experiment 1 under conditions where conceptual misinformation 
effects might be even greater.

recruited from the University of Chicago and surrounding community with the same 
inclusion criteria as Experiment 1.
Stimuli
The stimuli were identical to those used in Experiment 1.
Procedure
The experiment was identical to Experiment 1 except the 24-hour delay was introduced 
immediately after the encoding phase so that the misinformation phases always occurred on 
the second day (i.e., just prior to the retrieval phase).

Experiment 2 Results and Discussion
Full data for Experiment 2 can be found in Table 2. Our results sections follow the same 
format as Experiment 1. To anticipate these results, Experiment 2 replicated all of the key 
effects from Experiment 1 and also yielded stronger evidence that emotion can suppress the 
impact of conceptual fluency on recollection errors.
To investigate the impact of perceptual misinformation, we conducted a 3 (valence: negative, 
neutral, positive) × 2 (misinformation: perceptual, conceptual) repeated measures ANOVA 
(Figure 2a). There was a main effect of misinformation (F(1, 31) = 44.526, p < .001, 
ηP2 = . 590), as perceptual misinformation increased false alarms above the conceptual 
misinformation baseline. Second, this effect did not interact with valence (F(2, 62) = 1.793,

categories. Finally, there was a main effect of valence (F(2, 62) = 4.571, p = .014,

As in Experiment 1, this effect is consistent with an emotional distinctiveness heuristic.

Between-Experiments Comparisons
In Experiments 1 and 2 we found evidence that perceptual recombination and conceptual 
fluency increased recollection errors across all emotional categories. Although the 
perceptual recombination effect was robust for all emotional categories in both experiments, 
there was evidence in both experiments that the conceptual misinformation effect was 
reduced for emotional items, especially negative items, relative to neutral items. The 
interaction associated with this effect was marginal in Experiment 1 and significant in 
Experiment 2. To directly test differences between the timing of misinformation and increase 
power in detecting emotional interactions, we did a combined analysis of Experiments 1 and 
2.

General Discussion
This study investigated the impact of two hypothetical mechanisms of memory construction
—perceptual recombination and conceptual fluency—on the accuracy of neutral and 
emotional recollections. Here we highlight three key findings from each of the two 
experiments. First, we found evidence that the two mechanisms increased recollection 
errors, using different pictorial materials and other modifications of the Doss et al. (2016) 
procedure, thereby replicating those effects and demonstrating the robust nature of these two 
mechanisms across the emotional categories. Perceptual recombination, in particular, was 
found to drive high confidence errors, implicating a role in creating vivid false recollections. 
Second, in each experiment, we found that the size of the perceptual misinformation effect

on recollection errors was of similar magnitude across the emotional categories. Third, we 
found that the size of the conceptual fluency effect was reduced for negative (and to some 
degree positive) compared to neutral items, especially when the conceptual fluency 
manipulation occurred just prior to memory retrieval. This finding was consistent with the 
use of an emotional distinctiveness heuristic, as was the overall lower recollection error rate 
for negative compared to neutral lures. Below we elaborate on the theoretical significance of 
each of these findings.
Independent from our emotional manipulations, our experiments provide a conceptual 
replication of several key findings from Doss et al. (2016). False alarms were elevated for 
lures that had been associated with perceptual misinformation compared to the conceptual 
fluency baseline, suggesting a perceptual recombination effect on false recollection. 
Moreover, false alarms were elevated for lures that had been associated with conceptual 
fluency relative to the nonstudied foil baseline, suggesting a conceptual fluency effect on 
false recollection. Similar to Doss et al., we also found that the perceptual misinformation 
effect was not affected by the timing of the misinformation (i.e., pre- or post-delay), 
although evidence was mixed with respect to the extent that conceptual misinformation had 
a larger effect when administered post-delay compared to pre-delay. In addition to extending 
these effects to a different set of materials, the current experiments demonstrate that mental 
imagery of pictures during the initial study phase is unnecessary to obtain these effects, as 
we eliminated this aspect of the Doss et al. procedure.
With respect to the impact of emotion on these effects, we found that the perceptual 
misinformation effect was a similar magnitude across all emotional categories, but the size 
of the conceptual misinformation effect varied with emotionality. In Experiment 2, we found 
a reduced conceptual misinformation effect for both negative and positive items, and the 
combined analysis found a reduced effect for negative items. This effect implicates a 
reduced conceptual fluency effect for emotional items compared to neutral items, as 
predicted by the distinctiveness heuristic hypothesis and consistent with some of the results 
of Choi et al (2013). The lack of a similar reduction of the perceptual misinformation effect 
suggests that this effect may benefit less from a distinctiveness heuristic than the conceptual 
fluency effect, potentially because the presentation of fragmented pictures provides more 
perceptually detailed false recollections that mitigates the logic of a distinctiveness heuristic 
(i.e., “I would remember a perceptually detailed picture if it had been studied.”). By 
contrast, there was no evidence for the emotional enhancement hypothesis or the idea that 
emotional misinformation might be better remembered than neutral misinformation and 
hence have a larger effect on either of the two constructive mechanisms.
Related to the idea of an emotional distinctiveness heuristic, in each experiment we also 
found an overall tendency for fewer false alarms to negative lures, including those that had 
been associated with perceptual misinformation, conceptual misinformation, or no 
misinformation (nonstudied foils). Each of these effects is consistent with reduced impact of 
concept fluency or familiarity on negative false recollections, potentially owing to a 
distinctiveness heuristic because all lures could have been somewhat affected by concept 
fluency including nonstudied foils that could be familiar from idiosyncratic conceptual or 
semantic associations across items. Positive items did not consistently show this effect,

potentially because these items were less arousing than negative items and/or because they 
were more easily envisioned, as demonstrated in the analysis of clarity ratings for scrambled 
pictures from the perceptual misinformation phase (see SM). The overall impact of a 
distinctiveness heuristic may also have been greater for negative items because these items 
elicited stronger recollections as demonstrated by a concurrent increase in hit rates and 
recollection accuracy.
To summarize, we found evidence that both perceptual recombination and conceptual 
fluency can drive recollection errors of neutral and emotional events. We also found 
evidence that an emotional distinctiveness heuristic can mitigate the impact of conceptual 
fluency on recollection errors, but there was no evidence that an emotional distinctiveness 
heuristic affected perceptual recombination. Outside of the lab, these results suggest that 
detailed false recollections of negative events are more difficult to implant based on hearsay 
or misleading statements alone, compared to the presentation of perceptual misinformation, 
though certainly not impossible, as the effects of conceptual fluency were still quite apparent 
regardless of how a memory is emotionally charged. These results indicate that emotion can 
differentially impact the different constructive mechanisms that drive episodic recollection, 
and they help to explain why emotion sometimes enhances and other times diminishes 
memory distortion.
